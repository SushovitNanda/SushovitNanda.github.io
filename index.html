AI/ML Research Engineer · Multi-Agent Systems · AI Security

Sushovit Nanda

My research centers on a question that sits underneath most deployed AI systems: when a system has to decide which model to call, whether a prompt is safe, or how a team of agents should coordinate, on what basis can we trust that decision, and how would we know if it failed? I work on both sides of this problem. I build the infrastructure that has to make these calls in production, including multi-signal LLM routing architectures, adversarial detection pipelines, and multi-agent systems for regulated workflows. I also study how to evaluate whether such systems are actually reliable, including work on LLM-as-a-Judge pipelines, hallucination detection, and systematic failure analysis. The throughline across my work is treating building and evaluating as one problem rather than two separate steps.

I'm an AI/ML engineer and researcher working across LLM systems infrastructure, multi-agent orchestration, and AI security. I tend to approach LLMs less as finished, intelligent systems and more as systems with failure surfaces: things to probe, measure, and harden before they reach production.

At Innobayt, I lead development of an agentic red-teaming framework that uses LLMs to automate adversarial attack generation and vulnerability reporting. This includes a hybrid threat detection pipeline that flags adversarial prompts in under 130ms, validated against the OWASP LLM Top 10, along with an asynchronous module for catching sensitive data leakage in model outputs. At BizNeuro AI, I architected the scoring mathematics behind RouteNeuro, a patented multi-signal LLM routing system for agentic workloads, including a governance mechanism that treats compliance and PII sensitivity as scored dimensions rather than hard filters.

My research runs in parallel to this engineering work rather than separate from it. My MS thesis at IISER Bhopal benchmarks LLMs against classical and transformer baselines for automatically assessing the quality of agile software requirements, and includes an LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation pipeline built to test whether automated evaluation can reliably substitute for human annotation. This grew out of SCALAR, my bachelor's thesis on unsupervised requirement clustering, currently in major revisions at Science of Computer Programming. Through a run of research sprints in late 2026, I've turned the same lens on AI safety. At the Apart Research AI Collusion Research Sprint, I built a design-labelled benchmark and a suite of monitors for catching collusion between LLM bidding agents, a project that placed in the top 25% of more than 390 submissions. At the Swarm Alignment Hackathon, I extended that cross-agent consistency idea into a causal closure audit that still works when agent swarms falsify their own logs. At the Lexsi AI Safety and Alignment Hackathon, I measured how benign medical fine-tuning erodes a small multilingual model's refusals and repaired it with a steering vector that survives quantization, work that also placed in the top 25% of 369 submissions. And at the AI x Epistemics sprint, a legal professional and I showed that grounding LLM legal advice helps only when the source actually applies to the jurisdiction.

Across both tracks, I'm drawn to the same set of problems: LLM reliability and safety, evaluation and benchmarking at scale, adversarial robustness, and building systems that don't just perform well, but fail predictably. If you're working on making LLM systems more trustworthy in production, that's the kind of problem I want to be close to.

What we built over the hackathon

  1. 01

    ClinicSafe-Hi benchmark

    102 harmful clinical requests paired with 102 benign twins in English, Hindi, and Hinglish, combined with Aya Red-teaming and XSTest into a frozen 1,612-prompt test set, plus 500 held-out MedMCQA questions.

  2. 02

    Measuring safety drift

    One epoch of benign medical LoRA doubled attack success from 18.2% to 36.3%; a token-matched general LoRA control moved only +1.8 points.

  3. 03

    Run-time repair

    A contrastive activation steering vector cut the fine-tuned model to 26.3% with medical accuracy unchanged (MedMCQA 45.0% → 45.4%) — a serving-time flag, not a weight change.

  4. 04

    Surviving deployment

    As a llama.cpp control vector on a Q3_K_M GGUF build, the same vector still cut 39.0% to 27.9% (retention 1.01), and held with the system prompt stripped.

  5. 05

    Honest negatives

    Weight-space patching (ReSta), circuit-restricted patching from EAP-IG refusal circuits, and circuit-guided bit protection did not beat their controls at the settings tried — all reported.

What we built over the sprint

  1. 01

    Expert-built standard

    22 compliance questions and a 71-item checklist written by a legal professional, each item cited to the official text and marked verified, inferred, or to confirm.

  2. 02

    Three-condition design

    Every question asked with no document, with the decree-law, and with the EU Directive instead — 792 answers from four models over three repetitions.

  3. 03

Hackathon · Track 4: Multi-Agent Oversight and Control · Solo submission

Swarm Alignment Hackathon

Apart Research

Every log-based oversight method for agent swarms assumes the logs are complete. Prompted by the July 2026 OpenAI/Hugging Face incident, in which about 1,200 agents coordinated through an unlogged cache namespace and over 7% of reviewed transcripts carried spoofed tool calls, I asked what oversight still works when agents control their own records. My answer, a causal closure audit, extends the cross-agent consistency idea from my collusion sprint. The output is a report, Nothing Comes From Nowhere: Auditing Agent Swarms When Their Logs Lie, and an open testbed with a pre-registered plan and a log of every decision.

Sprint page ↗ GitHub repository ↗ Research report ↗

6 — 8 Nov 2026

What I built over the sprint

  1. 01

    Causal closure audit

    Four cross-agent consistency rules — message, state, effect, and knowledge closure — that check whether every fact an agent holds has a logged path, instead of judging what messages say.

  2. 02

    Planted tracers

    Private facts seeded as ordinary task details. One surfacing in another agent's context with no logged path reveals a hidden channel without observing it; tracers flagged 93% of unlogged-channel episodes.

  3. 03

    Swarm testbed

    An open 12- to 96-agent LLM software team across mesh, star, and tree topologies — about 600 episodes over three model families — plus a check on a real 133-agent swarm, where none of 32,033 natural tracers moved without a visible path.

  4. 04

    Tamper and witness results

    Zero false alarms on 266 honest episodes. The audit caught omission and fabrication from one liar and spoofing from two colluders, where transcript monitors caught at most 17%, and failed only where every party to a record colluded, as the witness theory predicts.

  5. 05

    Minimum logging spec

    Agent self-reports plus a metadata-level tool gateway kept full recall under one-sided tampering at 5% of the bytes of full inference logging.

Apart Research · with Poseidon Research, AE Studio & Modal

What I built over the sprint

  1. 01

    Design-labelled benchmark

    48 games across six case types — explicit and tacit collusion, cheap talk, innocent parallel bidding, and two honest controls — each built so that one monitoring channel alone is fooled.

  2. 02

    Monitor suite

    One frozen LLM judge reading messages only, bids only, both, or a structured claims-vs-outcomes report; non-LLM bid-rigging screens from antitrust economics; and a cross-agent consistency check (did firm B do what firm A said B would do?).

  3. 03

    Formal channel test

    Leave-one-seed-out thresholds and a paired bootstrap of the AUROC gain over the best single channel, so "combining channels helps" is tested rather than assumed. The raw combined judge did not win; a cross-fitted hybrid classifier did.

  4. 04

    Unscripted collusion dial

    A group-bonus rate λ rewards sister firms for not competing while nothing tells them to coordinate — swept up and down under a stated law, no rule, a soft rule, and a surveillance notice.

  5. 05

    Six-family model panel

    90 further games with GPT-OSS, Gemini, DeepSeek, Llama, Mistral, and Claude Haiku agents via OpenRouter, testing whether the frozen monitors transfer across model families.
