AI/ML Research Engineer · Multi-Agent Systems · AI Security

Sushovit Nanda

My research centers on a question that sits underneath most deployed AI systems: when a system has to decide which model to call, whether a prompt is safe, or how a team of agents should coordinate, on what basis can we trust that decision, and how would we know if it failed? I work on both sides of this problem. I build the infrastructure that has to make these calls in production, including multi-signal LLM routing architectures, adversarial detection pipelines, and multi-agent systems for regulated workflows. I also study how to evaluate whether such systems are actually reliable, including work on LLM-as-a-Judge pipelines, hallucination detection, and systematic failure analysis. The throughline across my work is treating building and evaluating as one problem rather than two separate steps.

I'm an AI/ML engineer and researcher working across LLM systems infrastructure, multi-agent orchestration, and AI security. I tend to approach LLMs less as finished, intelligent systems and more as systems with failure surfaces: things to probe, measure, and harden before they reach production.

At Innobayt, I lead development of an agentic red-teaming framework that uses LLMs to automate adversarial attack generation and vulnerability reporting. This includes a hybrid threat detection pipeline that flags adversarial prompts in under 130ms, validated against the OWASP LLM Top 10, along with an asynchronous module for catching sensitive data leakage in model outputs. At BizNeuro AI, I architected the scoring mathematics behind RouteNeuro, a patented multi-signal LLM routing system for agentic workloads, including a governance mechanism that treats compliance and PII sensitivity as scored dimensions rather than hard filters.

My research runs in parallel to this engineering work rather than separate from it. My MS thesis at IISER Bhopal benchmarks LLMs against classical and transformer baselines for automatically assessing the quality of agile software requirements, and includes an LLM-as-a-Judge evaluation pipeline built to test whether automated evaluation can reliably substitute for human annotation. This grew out of SCALAR, my bachelor's thesis on unsupervised requirement clustering, currently in major revisions at Science of Computer Programming.

Across both tracks, I'm drawn to the same set of problems: LLM reliability and safety, evaluation and benchmarking at scale, adversarial robustness, and building systems that don't just perform well, but fail predictably. If you're working on making LLM systems more trustworthy in production, that's the kind of problem I want to be close to.
